---

I've spent this whole series pulling on one thread: A classification is not a fact about information but a statement that the information once met a category of risk. This research started because a lot of the work I do with customers is talking to them about managing risk, and moving to a risk-based approach when thinking about the security systems we design to protect sensitive information.

I assumed some nation or some framework within a nation would say something to the effect of: "protect this only as long as it is actually worth protecting, and then stop". A unit's position on a battlefield is lethal for a few minutes/hours/days/weeks/months and worthless after that time period has passed. A number in a budget matters until the announcement and never again. Plenty of secrets have a short, honest shelf life, and I was really searching for some nation that's done the hard thinking about how to ratify this to a set of rules..

Taking cryptoperiods as a starting point, NIST SP 800-57 defines one as "the time span during which a specific key is authorized for use", and it names "the security life of the data" as a first-class factor, giving the range as "10 minutes, 10 days, or 10 years" (the actual number depends on the algorithm and on how much traffic the key protects). That is protection expressed as a literal span of time, which is exactly what I was hunting for. Or take [Executive Order 13526](https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html), which requires the original classifier to set a declassification date or event "based on the duration of the national security sensitivity of the information", and states flatly that "no information may remain classified indefinitely". Or take Michele Mosca's inequality for the quantum threat, which I wrote about last time, where the security shelf-life of the data is a named variable in the sum.

So the life of a secret seems to be pretty well understood, quantified, and engineered.. It just lives one layer down from the classification label, in key management, in doctrine, in a metadata field, rather than the layer that _I want it to be at_..

The principle of protect it for *long enough* is the thought here - The cryptoperiod worry is that the key might expire before the secret does. The quantum worry is that data harvested today stays valuable long enough to be decrypted in twenty years, so you must protect it for longer.. 

None of them says the other thing: protect it only as *briefly* as the value lasts, and relax when the value is gone. Or more specifically what I want in doctrine, which is "Start with the highest possible protections, then they can be gradually relaxed over time". I almost feel like I want to assign a monetary or points value to a secret, then we can easily compare mathematically a set of controls against a secret.

Several Baltic states protect certain sensitive information for around seventy-five years. Poland can protect the identity of an intelligence source essentially indefinitely. Those are enormous, deliberate commitments to durability, and they make sense: a source's safety does not expire on a schedule, what about their family, or their family's family - ..but hold that next to the other end of the range - nowhere is there an equivalent rule that allows us to step protection *down* for something that everyone agrees is worthless by next week. 

Some of the plumbing that I think I want already exists in public standards. I've written before about NATO's [ADatP-4774 and 4778](https://nso.nato.int), the pair that lets you attach a machine-readable confidentiality label to data and then bind it cryptographically so it cannot be silently stripped or altered. The security travels *with* the data. And 4774 already carries temporal fields: a creation time, a review time, and a "succession handling" element that can name the label which will *replace* the current one at a future date. In other words, the label format can already describe a planned change of protection over time - ..what is missing is the decision layer: a principled way to say how long the value actually lasts, and the confidence to let protection drop when it does.

The tension between the efficiency of matching protection to value and the asymmetry of the risk is where the really interesting thought lives for me.. The answer, it turns out (until I get my way at least), is that everybody half does it? We instrument the life of a secret all the time. We just only ever build the half that keeps things safe for longer. Building the other half, carefully, with a human hand on the irreversible switch, is where I think we can gain a level of operational value and speed that can genuinely give us an upper hand.
