---
title: "How Long Does a Secret Need to Stay Secret?"
date: 2026-08-31T09:00:00+00:00
draft: false
---

Someone who copies your encrypted traffic today and can't decrypt it, doesn't necessarily have to give up on that.. They can just keep it (because storage is cheap), and if the encryption protecting it is broken ten years from now, they can come back and read the whole lot then, at their leisure. The security term for this "harvest now, decrypt later" adds nuance to the question you have to ask about a secret: from whether anyone can read it today to how long it has to stay unreadable..

The reason that's suddenly a live worry rather than a thought experiment is the promise of "quantum computing". A large enough quantum computer (if and when one actually turns up) would break the public-key cryptography that almost everything currently leans on (the padlock in your browser, more or less). Michele Mosca, who works on exactly this, [wrote the problem down](https://eprint.iacr.org/2015/1075) as a short inequality with three numbers in it. _x_ is the security shelf-life of your data, how long it has to stay secret. _y_ is your migration time, how long it takes you to move everything over to quantum-safe cryptography. _z_ is how long until a quantum computer can break what you're using now. His rule is just that "if x + y > z, we have a serious problem today", because anything you protect over the next _y_ years can be harvested and then broken within _x_ years of that, while it still matters. His own guess at _z_, and he is clear that it is a guess, was a fifty-fifty chance of breaking RSA-2048 by 2031.

What I find interesting is that _x_, the life of the secret, is a term in the equation at all. Mosca even puts numbers on it: "0 years for applications requiring only real-time security", running up to "10, 20, 100 years when protecting your personal health information, trade secrets, or national security information". This is, as far as I've found, about the only place in the whole business where how long a secret has to live is an actual number feeding an actual decision, rather than a vague sense that some things matter for longer than others. A classification level, as I keep saying, is a judgement about _how bad_ a compromise would be; this is one of the very few times anyone puts a figure on _how long_.

And it seems to have turned into a government instruction! NIST in the US, the NCSC in the UK, France's ANSSI and Germany's BSI have all published post-quantum migration guidance, and they say more or less the same thing: work out how long each kind of data has to stay confidential, and migrate the long-lived material first. The NCSC [tells organisations](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/guidance/pqc-migration-timelines) in as many words to record each dataset's "expected lifetime" and to start with their "most valuable or long-lived data". [NIST's timeline](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2024/NIST.IR.8547.ipd.pdf), as currently drafted, deprecates its weaker parameters after 2030 and disallows the vulnerable algorithms entirely after 2035, and notes that systems "with long-term confidentiality needs" may have to move earlier than that.

All of it runs in one direction: the longer a secret has to live, the sooner and the harder you protect it. Mosca's `x = 0` case is there (the secret that only needs to survive the next few seconds) but nobody turns it into "so protect this one less".. Even here, in the one corner of the field that actually went and put a number on the life of a secret, the number only ever ratchets protection upward. Protecting something only as briefly as it is genuinely worth protecting, and then deliberately stopping, still isn't a thing anyone writes down.
